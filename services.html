What I do

It starts with knowing what you have. From there: collect it better, put it to work, cut what you don't need, and make it last. Most organizations need two or three of these, and engagements usually start small and only continue if the first piece proves useful.

Service 01

Find out what you actually have

Before anything gets rebuilt, someone has to inventory the mess. I go through your drives, databases, and systems and produce a plain-language picture of what exists, what's duplicated, what's wrong, and what you're at risk of losing.

  • Inventory of every dataset, where it lives, and who touches it
  • What's authoritative versus what's an abandoned copy
  • Where you're exposed: single points of failure, undocumented processes, no backups
  • A prioritized list of what's worth fixing, with the cheap wins called out first
Service 02

Set your team up to collect its own data

Most small organizations are paying someone to re-type field notes into a spreadsheet. I stand up an ArcGIS Online organization and build the collection tools around it, so your crews capture what they see on a phone or tablet and it lands in one place, already correct. I've built field collection systems for Washington State fisheries research and worked field seasons myself, so the forms are designed by someone who has filled them out in the rain.

  • ArcGIS Online organization set up from scratch, with users, groups, permissions, and licensing sized to your team
  • Field data collection forms your crews can actually use, with validation that stops bad entries at the source
  • Offline collection for the places with no signal, syncing when they're back
  • Dashboards so office staff can see what came in without asking anyone
Service 03

Custom applications and web maps

When the off-the-shelf template doesn't fit, I build the thing you actually need. I'm a developer as well as a GIS analyst, so the option isn't limited to what a configuration wizard will allow, whether that's inside the Esri ecosystem or a standalone web app with no licensing attached to it at all.

  • Public-facing maps and portals for residents, members, boards, or grant reporting
  • Internal tools that fit how your staff actually work, not how software assumes they do
  • Live data applications that pull from public feeds and refresh on their own
  • Print and presentation cartography when the deliverable is a map, not an app
Service 04

Make sense of the data you've been collecting for years

Organizations accumulate decades of records and rarely get to ask them anything. I go through what you have and find the patterns: where failures cluster, what's changed over time, which assets are trending the wrong direction, what the data supports when you're writing a grant application or making a case to a board.

  • Trend and pattern analysis across years of operational, asset, or monitoring records
  • Spatial analysis: what's near what, what overlaps, what's exposed
  • Remote sensing and LiDAR analysis for vegetation, terrain, and habitat questions
  • Findings written in plain language, with the maps and figures you need to present them
Service 05

Get you off infrastructure you don't need

A full ArcGIS Enterprise deployment is a lot of license and a lot of server for an organization of fifteen people. In many cases the same work runs on ArcGIS Online plus open-source tooling for a fraction of the cost and a fraction of the maintenance. I've managed ArcGIS Enterprise at multiple organizations, so I know what it's worth when an organization needs it and what it costs when one doesn't. I assess honestly whether that's true for you, including when it isn't, and run the migration if it is.

  • Straight comparison of what you pay now versus what the alternatives cost
  • Migration to ArcGIS Online, PostGIS, QGIS, or a mix, depending on what fits
  • Your data moved over intact, with nothing stranded on a server nobody logs into
  • No server for your staff to patch, back up, or renew
Service 06

Make it survive staff turnover

The most common failure I see isn't a bad system. It's a good one that only one person understood. Whatever we build, I document it so the next person can pick it up without calling me.

  • Written documentation in your team's language, not GIS jargon
  • Naming conventions and standards simple enough to actually get followed
  • A clear owner and a routine for keeping data current
  • Training for the staff who'll run it day to day

Service 07

Records, data, and SharePoint

Shared drives that grew for fifteen years without a plan, a SharePoint site nobody can find anything in, or boxes of paper that should have been digital a decade ago. I design the structure, move the content, and set the rules that keep it from sliding back.

  • Administrative workflows organized, documented, and moved off paper and email
  • SharePoint site architecture, permissions, and governance standards
  • Paper-to-digital records conversion with retrieval in seconds instead of days
  • Retention, naming, and metadata rules that make records findable and compliant
  • Cleanup of duplicated, orphaned, and outdated content on shared drives
  • Training for the staff who have to live in it

  • Budget, contract, and service-level management for the systems you depend on
